\title{Simulating Political Discourse on Twitter/X: A Large Language Model-Integrated Agent-Based Model for Forward-Looking Thread Generation}
\author{Luke Tervit}
\course{Computer Science}
\project{4th Year Project Report}

\date{\today}

\abstract{
This dissertation presents a large language model (LLM)-integrated agent-based model (ABM) pipeline that generates synthetic political reply threads from a root tweet using agent profiles estimated from users' past tweets in USC~X~24, a large collection of Twitter/X tweets and reply threads from the 2024 US presidential election period. USC~X~24 was chosen because it provides both politically salient reply threads and the user-history data needed to construct empirically grounded agent profiles; we used the May--July 2024 shard (parts~1--2) as a contiguous arbitrary subset, approximately equivalent to a random slice of the full corpus, because processing the entire dataset was infeasible within the available compute budget. Across 100 held-out threads, the system achieved mean overall accuracy of $92.3\% \pm 4.6\%$ and a composite fidelity score, a weighted summary of thread-level realism, of $0.857 \pm 0.106$, with no significant directional sentiment bias and strongest agreement on political composition, where the mean Jensen--Shannon Divergence (JSD), a measure of how different two distributions are, was 0.039.
}

\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Motivation}
Engagement metrics for political content on X, such as likes, reposts, and reply counts, are easy to collect and useful for descriptive analysis. They are weak proxies for reaction dynamics such as stance evolution, affective response, and hostility patterns~\citep{diehl2016political,bozdag2025survey}. This creates a practical gap. Analysts can observe what content received attention, but they cannot directly test how similar populations may react to a new tweet before posting.

We address that gap with a forward-looking simulation pipeline. Given only a root tweet and a population of agents whose profiles are estimated from past tweets, the system generates a synthetic reply thread. It then evaluates realism against observed threads using distributional fidelity metrics, that is, measures of how closely the synthetic thread matches the real one~\citep{composta2025simulating,butler2024misinformation}. Throughout this dissertation, a \emph{tweet} is an individual Twitter/X message, while a \emph{reply thread} is the root tweet together with the replies that accumulate beneath it. We therefore use \emph{thread} only for the conversation as a whole, not for a single tweet. The aim is not to recover hidden mental states such as beliefs, intentions, or feelings that cannot be observed directly. It is to reproduce observable reaction patterns in language closely enough to support controlled studies and bounded pre-posting message analysis.

\section{Limitations of Current Approaches}
Current approaches often fall into two categories. First, predictive content models estimate engagement or label-level outcomes from historical features, but they do not generate conversational trajectories. Second, classical opinion-dynamics agent-based models (ABMs) represent agents with continuous or scalar opinions rather than generated language. In these models, agents update numeric opinions through simple rules, such as bounded-confidence dynamics where they respond mainly to others within a similarity threshold~\citep{deffuant2000mixing,hegselmann2002opinion}. That makes direct comparison with real social media discourse difficult.

As a result, there is limited work that can simultaneously (i) run in a strictly forward mode from a root tweet, (ii) generate realistic natural-language replies, and (iii) support quantitative validation against real thread-level distributions across sentiment, politics, emotion, and aggression.

\section{Core Thesis Claim}
The core claim of this dissertation is that a large language model (LLM)-integrated agent-based model (ABM) can function as a \emph{new research instrument} for political communication. It does more than describe past platform behaviour. It enables controlled forward simulation of likely reply dynamics for unseen root tweets. This shifts the methodological unit of analysis from retrospective engagement correlation to prospective thread generation under explicit behavioural assumptions.

In practical terms, the pipeline enables study designs that are difficult or impossible with prior methods. These include counterfactual root-tweet edits that hold audience composition fixed, advance estimation of downstream sentiment and stance trajectories, and controlled re-tests of claims from observational papers. The same capability also has practical value for message-design workflows, where the goal is to compare likely discourse effects of alternative phrasings before publication under clearly stated assumptions.

\section{Project Objectives}
This dissertation has four objectives:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Build an LLM-integrated ABM that simulates reply-thread dynamics from a root tweet without using target-thread reply data.
\item Construct agent populations from historical user profiles so that simulation participants reflect empirical behavioural heterogeneity.
\item Evaluate simulation realism at scale on 100 held-out political threads using multi-metric fidelity analysis and statistical testing.
\item Compare model choices and behavioural design decisions through ablation experiments, where one design choice is changed at a time, and through cross-model experiments.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions}
This dissertation makes four main contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A novel forward-simulation pipeline}: a text-generating ABM+LLM system that predicts full reply-thread trajectories from a root tweet without access to example replies from the target thread during generation, enabling counterfactual studies and bounded message-testing workflows that engagement-only analytics and non-text ABMs cannot run.
\item \textbf{An empirically grounded agent construction workflow}: agent behavioural profiles are estimated from observed historical tweet data rather than invented by the researcher, producing simulation populations that preserve observed political and linguistic heterogeneity.
\item \textbf{A reproducible thread-level validation protocol}: 100-thread evaluation with per-thread metrics across sentiment, political stance, emotion, aggression, and volume, supporting direct comparison between synthetic and real discourse.
\item \textbf{Evidence of what makes the pipeline useful}: a 21-configuration ablation study, cross-model comparison (Dolphin-Llama3 8B vs Qwen2.5 7B), and counterfactual case study that isolate the design choices and use cases most responsible for its value.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Background and Relevant Work}

\section{Problem Framing: Engagement vs Reaction Dynamics}
Prior work has identified emotional tone, framing, and source cues as important correlates of political message spread, but most analyses remain retrospective and engagement-centric~\citep{bozdag2025survey,diehl2016political,antypas2023negativity,geels2024virtuallabcoats,dumas2022influencers}. These approaches explain what happened after publication, but they do not provide a controlled way to test likely downstream reply dynamics for unseen content.

This gap motivates simulation-oriented approaches that estimate observable reaction trajectories rather than hidden psychological states that cannot be measured directly~\citep{butler2024misinformation,composta2025simulating}. In this dissertation, reaction quality is measured through thread-level outcomes: sentiment and political distributions, emotion mix, aggression level, and reply volume. That framing matters. The pipeline is designed to answer ``what happens next if the root tweet changes?'' rather than ``what correlated with engagement last time?''

This chapter reviews existing research on LLM-based social simulation, online political discourse dynamics, and measurement limitations of engagement-only evaluation.

\section{Previous Work}

The simulation of social dynamics has a long history in agent-based modelling. Classical opinion-dynamics models treat agents as numeric opinion holders who revise their views through repeated interactions. Two standard examples are the bounded-confidence model~\citep{deffuant2000mixing}, where agents are influenced only by sufficiently similar others, and the Hegselmann--Krause model~\citep{hegselmann2002opinion}. These models explain convergence and polarisation well, but they do not produce text. Their outputs are therefore not directly comparable to real social media data.

More recently, large language models have been used to create agents that generate natural-language behaviour. Park et al.~\citep{park2023generative} showed that LLM-driven agents with detailed behavioural profiles can simulate believable human-like interactions in an open-ended sandbox environment. This established persona-conditioned generation as a viable approach to social simulation. In the political domain, Composta et al.~\citep{composta2025simulating} calibrated LLM agents on conversations from the 2022 Italian election and fitted activity patterns on Bluesky data. Their system generated content, built social network structure, and matched real in-group interaction patterns to a median correlation of 0.85.

The key difference in our pipeline is not only how those personas are constructed, but how they are exposed to the generator. Recent prompt-persona studies show that LLM behaviour is highly sensitive to role wording and descriptor choice: prompt formulation can materially change alignment, while poorly specified personas can degrade performance or stereotype the target group~\citep{kim2024persona,lutz2025prompt}. We therefore do not pass classifier outputs straight through as bare labels or scores. Instead, we derive each agent profile from historical user tweets classified by five Twitter-specialised models, then translate those outputs into behavioural instructions that the generator can actually enact. That gives the simulator a firmer empirical basis for political leaning, emotion, sentiment, and aggression before any generation step occurs, and places the method closer to the emerging state of the art in persona prompting than to older coarse-label role assignment.

These studies show that profile-conditioned generation is plausible. They stop short of the main goal of this dissertation. Many LLM simulation papers evaluate aggregate interaction structure rather than full thread text and thread-level fidelity. Many political-language systems focus on pairwise judgement or moderation tasks rather than end-to-end reply-thread simulation~\citep{ji2018coattention,mohammadi2025aifeedback}. We therefore build on that literature in a more specific direction: strict forward generation from a root tweet, full-thread text output, and thread-level quantitative validation.

\section{Reaction Mechanisms in Online Political Discourse}
Online political discourse often exhibits reinforcement and conversion-like patterns: users may become more entrenched in prior views or shift toward new framings depending on context, identity, and interaction structure~\citep{druckman2022framework,diehl2016political}. On platforms such as X, these processes play out through rapid public reply chains where language, affect, and visibility co-evolve over time.

This matters for the simulator because agent replies should depend on both stable persona tendencies and the local conversational state. That is why the pipeline combines history-informed agent profiles with round-based interaction and rolling thread context, rather than treating each reply as an isolated text-generation task.

\section{Measurement Challenges in Social Media Studies}
Research identifies several factors that shape large-scale reaction patterns on platforms such as X, including content structure, emotional tone, language style, and social proof mechanisms~\citep{brady2017emotion,geels2024virtuallabcoats,dumas2022influencers}.

Existing social media studies of political communication typically proxy downstream reaction with engagement metrics (likes, shares, comments), assuming that behavioural engagement indicates latent attitudinal response~\citep{diehl2016political}.

That assumption is too weak for the questions pursued here. If the goal is to compare alternative phrasings, test diffusion claims, or anticipate downstream tone before publication, the evaluation target has to be the reply thread itself. This is why the dissertation validates sentiment, politics, emotion, aggression, and volume directly instead of relying on engagement as a stand-in.

\section{Positioning of This Work}
The dissertation sits between descriptive social-media analytics and theoretical opinion-dynamics models. It uses an LLM-based ABM to generate thread text, then validates the result against real threads with explicit quantitative metrics. The aim is predictive simulation of observable reaction dynamics under clear modelling assumptions, not direct inference of internal psychological truth. In practical terms, the contribution is a validated pipeline for prospective and counterfactual thread-level experimentation. The later case study shows one of the clearest designs that this pipeline enables.

\chapter{Methodology}

\section{Research Design}

We adopt an agent-based modelling (ABM) approach because political reply threads are emergent systems. Thread-level sentiment, political mix, and reply volume arise from many local decisions about whether to reply, who to reply to, and what to say~\citep{diehl2016political,smith2021twitterbehaviors,smith2022politicalcontent}. ABM is therefore the right starting point. Rather than modelling the aggregate distribution directly, it models local interactions and lets thread-level outcomes emerge~\citep{axelrod1997complexity,epstein1996growing}.

The key methodological move is to integrate large language model (LLM) generation into the ABM agent loop. Classical opinion-dynamics ABMs represent agents as scalar opinion values updated by mathematical rules~\citep{deffuant2000mixing,hegselmann2002opinion}. That gives analytical simplicity, but no text output. It therefore prevents direct comparison with real social-media threads. By contrast, an LLM-conditioned ABM produces observable language that can be analysed with the same tools used on real threads~\citep{butler2024misinformation,composta2025simulating}. The trade-off is greater modelling complexity. That same complexity is what makes thread-level validation possible.

The research question is predictive: given only the content of a root tweet, can the system generate a realistic simulation of the reply thread that forms around it? That requirement forces a fully forward design: no real reply data from the target thread may be used as input. The pipeline must generalise from historical user behaviour to unseen root tweets, which is why evaluation matters as much as generation quality.

\section{Theoretical Framework}

\subsection{Bounded Confidence}

The bounded-confidence model~\citep{deffuant2000mixing} provides the theoretical basis for agent interaction selection. In this class of model, agents respond only to others whose views fall within a chosen distance threshold. Messages outside that threshold are ignored. In this dissertation, bounded confidence governs which tweets an agent is likely to engage with. Agents are more likely to target tweets from similar or moderately opposing political positions, and less likely to engage with ideologically extreme ones~\citep{diehl2016political,composta2025simulating}. The bounded-confidence threshold is a free parameter. We set it to 0.3 on the normalised [0, 1] political-distance scale. We chose 0.3 as a moderate cutoff. It is wide enough to permit cross-partisan interaction, which is common in the dataset, but narrow enough to stop target selection collapsing into near-random engagement across the full ideological range. We treat it as a calibrated working value, not a universal constant. More broadly, this is why the dissertation includes an ablation study. Free behavioural parameters are not assumed to be correct a priori; they are stress-tested by asking whether alternative settings improve downstream fidelity.

\subsection{The Backfire Effect}

The backfire effect~\citep{nyhan2010corrections} describes cases where contradictory information entrenches a strongly held belief rather than revising it. In the ABM, we implement this as a reply-targeting rule. Agents with high aggression scores (hate\_score + offensive\_score $>$ 0.7) who encounter tweets at extreme ideological distance ($>$ 0.6 on the political scale) are more likely to produce hostile replies than to move toward the opposing view~\citep{druckman2022framework,diehl2016political,brady2017emotion}. These cutoffs were chosen so that backfire-like behaviour remains a high-conflict exception rather than the default response. On the normalised scales used here, ideological distance above 0.6 marks clearly cross-cutting disagreement, while aggression above 0.7 isolates only the most hostile agents. This produces the partisan entrenchment and cross-ideological attack patterns seen in real political threads.

\subsection{Agent DNA as Behavioural Profile}

Each active agent is characterised by a DNA profile derived from historical Twitter activity. The profile includes political leaning (Left / Centre / Right), political confidence, dominant emotion, sentiment orientation, hate-speech likelihood, and offensive-language likelihood. We chose these dimensions because they capture the key axes along which political Twitter users differ in their likely behaviour~\citep{antypas2023negativity,tun2023sentiment,smith2022politicalcontent}. The hate and offensive scores are combined into a single aggression parameter. That parameter governs reply probability, tone-tier selection, and backfire-effect activation. Political leaning and its associated confidence score govern target selection and opinion distance calculation.

This DNA profile was produced by a bank of five specialised transformer classifiers applied at the tweet level: political leaning, emotion, sentiment, hate speech, and offensive language. Each model takes a single tweet as input and returns either a discrete label or a continuous score on its own dimension. Running all five models over a user's historical tweets therefore gives a behavioural representation grounded in observed language rather than researcher-written personas.

The aggregation step turns raw classifier outputs into a persona. For each user, categorical traits are assigned by majority behaviour. The political label, dominant emotion, and dominant sentiment are the modal labels across that user's historical tweets. Continuous traits are averaged across tweets. Political confidence is the mean political-classifier score, and hate and offensive scores are averaged separately before being combined into one user-level aggression scalar. Those user-level values are then mapped into prompt-facing descriptors. In practice, the modal political label selects the ideology block (Left/Centre/Right), the modal emotion label is converted through an emotion-to-behaviour lookup such as anger $\rightarrow$ ``furious and combative,'' and the continuous aggression score is bucketed into tone tiers such as casual, assertive, combative, or confrontational. This avoids forcing the LLM to infer the meaning of arbitrary numeric scales and produced more stable agent behaviour in development. Full model details are given in Phase~2.

\section{Data Source}

\subsection{USC X 24 Dataset}

The primary data source is the USC X 24 dataset, a large-scale collection of Twitter/X tweets gathered during the 2024 US presidential election period. The full published dataset contains over forty million tweets collected via the Twitter/X application programming interface (API) under academic research access. From that 20-part corpus, we used the May--July 2024 shard (parts 1--2), comprising approximately 2.3 million tweets with reply chains, view counts, and user metadata. We chose this subset as a contiguous and readily available partition of the corpus, not for content-based reasons. It should therefore be treated as roughly equivalent to a random subset of the full dataset. The period also provides dense, politically salient interaction and frequent cross-partisan exchange, making it suitable for both agent construction and held-out thread evaluation.

\subsection{Thread Selection}

We selected threads for simulation using two criteria. First, each thread needed enough replies to support meaningful ground-truth comparison; we required at least ten observed replies. Second, each thread needed some cross-partisan engagement, because ideologically homogeneous threads provide little leverage for testing bounded confidence and backfire-style mechanisms~\citep{diehl2016political,composta2025simulating,cetinkaya2025crosspartisan}. During development, we used a January 6th Capitol-attack thread as the main diagnostic case because it satisfied both criteria and made distributional errors easy to inspect.

For large-scale validation and ablation, we then drew 100 threads spanning varied topics, political compositions, and thread sizes. That broader sample tests whether the pipeline works beyond one illustrative thread and turns the dissertation from a case demo into an instrument evaluation.

\subsection{Agent Population Construction}

For each selected thread, we constructed the active agent population by matching the user IDs of thread participants against the classified agent DNA profiles derived from the full dataset. This produced a set of agents whose behavioural profiles were estimated from historical tweets rather than manually assumed. The political distribution, emotional register, and aggression levels of the agent population therefore reflected the actual users who participated in each thread. This is a key distinction from prior work, which often initialises agents with theoretically assumed distributions such as uniform or normal opinion distributions~\citep{composta2025simulating}. It also differs from prompt-only persona assignment. The prompt does not invent the persona from scratch; it translates a profile already estimated from historical behaviour.

\section{Simulation Design}

\subsection{Simultaneous Activation}

The simulation used a simultaneous (synchronous) activation schedule. In each round, agents are evaluated against the same current thread state, and only those that pass the reply-probability step generate pending replies. Those pending replies are then committed simultaneously. This prevents sequential activation bias, in which agents that act earlier in a round would see a different thread state from those that act later. Simultaneous activation therefore ensures that all responding agents act on the same conversational snapshot, which is consistent with the near-simultaneous nature of real social media engagement~\citep{butler2024misinformation}.

\subsection{Reply Probability and Target Selection}

An agent's probability of generating a reply in a given round was set as a base probability (5\%) modified by a linear function of the agent's aggression score, capped at a maximum. In implementation terms, each agent receives one Bernoulli trial per round with
\[
p(\text{reply}) = \min(0.25,\; 0.05 + 0.15 \times \text{aggression}),
\]
after which a uniform random draw determines whether that agent stays silent or proceeds to target selection. Agents therefore do not reply deterministically. Even highly aggressive agents may skip a round, while low-aggression agents still retain a small chance of speaking. We chose 5\% as a middle baseline between sparse and saturated participation. It is low enough to stop every agent speaking in every round, but high enough to let active threads develop. The linear aggression term captures the empirical observation that more hostile and emotionally activated users are more likely to engage in online political discourse~\citep{antypas2023negativity,brady2017emotion}. The 25\% cap prevents a small set of extreme agents from dominating every round by construction. If an agent passes this reply gate, it selects a target tweet from the current thread snapshot. If it fails, it takes no action that round.

Target selection is also weighted rather than deterministic. In each round, an agent first considers only a recent slice of the thread: tweets from the current or previous round, or failing that the most recent tweets in the thread. Each candidate tweet starts with weight 1.0. Tweets from the current round are multiplied by 3.0 so the simulator preferentially continues live exchanges rather than reviving stale parts of the thread. Ideology then changes that weight through political distance on a simple Left--Centre--Right scale mapped to 0, 0.5, and 1. For most agents, larger political distance increases the weight by a factor of $(1 + \text{distance})$, so mildly opposing tweets are often more attractive than perfectly aligned ones. Highly aggressive agents receive an additional controversy bonus when distance is large. This makes them more likely to seek out opponents. Low-aggression agents are penalised for very distant targets, so they are less likely to jump into extreme cross-partisan fights. Tweets deeper than five replies are also multiplied by 0.5. This discourages the simulation from overproducing long, isolated sub-threads.

The controversy weight parameter controlled the strength of the cross-partisan targeting tendency~\citep{cetinkaya2025crosspartisan}. Its default value was set to 2.5 as a moderate cross-partisan bonus between the ablated low setting (1.0) and high setting (5.0), so disagreement was encouraged without overwhelming topic relevance and recency.

\subsection{Text Generation}

Each agent's reply was generated by Dolphin-Llama3 8B and conditioned on a system prompt constructed from that agent's DNA profile. The system prompt described the agent's political orientation in terms of values and attitudes rather than categorical labels. It also included banks of politically charged vocabulary matched to the agent's ideology and derived from frequency analysis of the USC~X~24 tweet dataset. For example, right-leaning agents received cues such as ``MAGA,'' ``Trump,'' or ``border security,'' while left-leaning agents received cues such as ``Biden,'' ``democracy,'' or ``social justice.'' The prompt also assigned an aggression-appropriate tone tier scaled relative to the thread's mean aggression and included explicit behavioural rules preventing the model from identifying itself as an AI or echoing its own prompt structure. The user prompt contained two components: the eight most recent tweets in the simulated thread as conversational context, and the specific tweet being replied to. No real reply text from the target thread was injected into generation prompts.

\section{Validation Methodology}

\subsection{Classification of Generated Text}

All generated tweets were classified through the same five RoBERTa-based text classifiers used to construct the original agent DNA profiles: political leaning, emotion, sentiment, hate speech, and offensive language. This keeps the measurement pipeline consistent between real and simulated data, reducing the risk that apparent agreement or disagreement is caused by changing evaluators rather than changing text~\citep{antypas2023negativity,tun2023sentiment}. The trade-off is that classifier bias affects both construction and evaluation, which we treat later as a validity threat, that is, a factor that may weaken how confidently the results can be interpreted, rather than hiding it.

\subsection{Distributional Similarity via Jensen--Shannon Divergence}
\label{sec:jsd_method}

For political leaning, emotion, and sentiment, we compared the simulated and real thread-level label distributions using Jensen--Shannon Divergence (JSD). We use JSD because it is symmetric and remains well-defined when one thread assigns zero probability to a category, which is common in short reply chains. Lower values therefore indicate closer agreement between the simulated and real distributions~\citep{apellaniz2024synthetic}. Throughout the dissertation, we treat JSD values below 0.15 as a conservative, study-specific benchmark for close match. This is not a field-standard cutoff. We chose 0.15 because it remains firmly in the low-divergence regime on the JSD scale: since the theoretical maximum is $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$, a value of 0.15 is only about 22\% of the full range. In practical terms, this makes the benchmark strict enough that clearly mismatched distributions do not count as good agreement, while still allowing for the sampling noise and classifier uncertainty that are unavoidable in short political reply threads.

\subsection{Continuous Sentiment Score and Bias Testing}

In addition to the categorical sentiment label, we derived a continuous sentiment score from the raw model probability outputs: $P(\text{positive}) - P(\text{negative})$, yielding a value on $[-1, +1]$. This continuous score enabled per-thread paired comparison between real and simulated mean sentiment, and the computation of the sentiment residual (simulated mean $-$ real mean) as an indicator of directional bias.

To test for systematic bias across the full set of threads, we applied a Wilcoxon signed-rank test to the per-thread sentiment residuals. This test checks whether the typical paired difference is consistently above or below zero across threads. We selected the Wilcoxon test over the paired $t$-test because it makes no assumption of normality in the distribution of residuals, which cannot be guaranteed given the diversity of thread types and sizes. A statistically significant result ($p < 0.05$) indicates that the simulation exhibits a consistent directional bias across threads; a non-significant result indicates that observed differences are consistent with random variation.

\subsection{Weighted Overall Accuracy}

An overall accuracy score was computed as a weighted combination of the three JSD-based similarity scores and the normalised aggression gap:

\begin{equation}
\begin{array}{l}
\text{Overall} = 0.30 \times (1 - \text{Political JSD}) + 0.20 \times (1 - \text{Emotion JSD}) \\
\qquad\quad + 0.30 \times (1 - \text{Sentiment JSD}) + 0.20 \times (1 - \text{normalised aggression gap})
\end{array}
\end{equation}

The weights are deliberately unequal. Political and sentiment similarity receive the largest weights (0.30 each) because they correspond to the two main thread-level outcomes this dissertation is trying to reproduce: partisan composition and overall affective tone. Emotion and aggression receive slightly lower weights (0.20 each) because they are less direct measures in this setting. Emotion is a finer-grained, higher-variance multi-class problem. Aggression is an inferred proxy derived from hate-speech and offensive-language scores rather than a native ground-truth label. The weighting therefore reflects both substantive importance and measurement confidence rather than assuming that all four components are equally informative.

Sentiment and political alignment were weighted equally as the primary behavioural dimensions; emotion and aggression calibration were weighted equally as secondary dimensions. Threads were rated EXCELLENT ($\geq 80\%$), GOOD (60--79\%), FAIR (40--59\%), or POOR ($< 40\%$). These bands are descriptive reporting bins rather than inferential thresholds; they were chosen to make the 100-thread results readable at a glance.

\section{Experimental Design}

\subsection{Pipeline Validation (100 Threads)}

The first experiment asks a simple question: does the redesigned pipeline work at all beyond the development thread? We answer that with a 100-thread batch run. This is not a tuning exercise; parameters are held constant so the result reflects pipeline viability, not search over settings. We pushed evaluation to 100 threads because this was the largest batch that remained feasible within the available time and compute budget while still allowing full downstream classification, figure generation, and manual audit of failures. A larger held-out set would strengthen external-validity claims further, but would have materially increased runtime.

\subsection{Parameter Optimisation}

The second experiment asks whether the most serious failure mode can be repaired without breaking the rest of the system. After identifying systematic negative sentiment bias in the initial pipeline (mean residual $-0.390$, Wilcoxon $p = 4.27 \times 10^{-18}$), we implement the coordinated prompt changes described in Chapter~4 and re-run validation on the same 100-thread corpus to measure the reduction in bias and the effect on overall fidelity.

\subsection{Ablation Study}

The third experiment asks which design choices actually matter. To answer that, we run a 21-configuration ablation study. Each configuration is either a single-parameter departure from the optimised baseline or one of five multi-parameter combinations. The single-parameter ablations vary temperature, context window size, aggression tier design, reply probability, controversy weight, vocabulary injection, repeat penalty, response length, and simulation length. Each configuration is evaluated across 10 threads, yielding 210 total simulations. We rank configurations with a composite score combining sentiment JSD, temporal decay, lexical diversity (type-token ratio, TTR), semantic drift, emotion trajectory, the Gini coefficient (a measure of concentration), Spearman rank correlation (a rank-order correlation measure), and stance consistency. Each metric is first normalised to a common $[0,1]$ scale, and the final ablation score is the arithmetic mean. This means the ranking reflects balanced performance across several failure modes rather than one metric dominating by scale alone. We use this broader composite because ablations need to detect more than simple distributional mismatch. Sentiment JSD captures tonal realism, temporal decay captures activity-shape realism, TTR captures redundancy, semantic drift checks topic retention, emotion trajectory checks affective evolution, the Gini coefficient checks whether participation is overly concentrated, Spearman correlation checks thread-level aggression calibration, and stance consistency penalises persona drift.

This follows standard controlled ablation logic from natural language processing (NLP) and machine learning, adapted to an ABM setting where ``performance'' is not a single loss value but similarity to observed discourse along several dimensions.

\section{Ethical Considerations}

All tweet data used in this study was collected under academic research access to the Twitter/X application programming interface (API) and forms part of the publicly available USC X 24 dataset. No personally identifiable information beyond user IDs (which are public) was used in the analysis. Agent DNA profiles aggregate behaviour across many tweets rather than representing individual posts, further reducing the risk of identifying specific users from model outputs. The simulation generates entirely synthetic text; no generated content is attributed to real individuals. The use of an uncensored language model to generate realistic hostile political language was judged necessary for scientific validity --- a model that cannot reproduce the hostility of real discourse cannot serve as a valid simulation instrument --- and all generated content remained within the closed research environment.

\section{Phase 1: Initial Prototype Using a General-Purpose Language Model}

The initial approach used a single general-purpose large language model, Anthropic's Claude Haiku (\texttt{claude-haiku-4-5-20251001}), to perform all three pipeline stages. It classified tweet content into agent personality profiles, generated synthetic agent responses, and evaluated the resulting simulated threads. Approximately one million tweets from the USC X 24 dataset were submitted to the Claude API for classification in a single pass. This yielded a set of agent personality profiles that included political orientation and inferred behavioural tendencies. These profiles were then used to initialise synthetic agents, which were exposed to high-engagement tweets and prompted to generate responses.

This prototype proved fundamentally inadequate when compared with real thread data. Across three separate experiments, no agent changed its opinion following any interaction. Opinion scores were identical before and after every simulated exchange. Engagement was also minimal. Only two comments were generated across the full set of experiments, and no nested reply chains formed at any depth. More critically, the simulated outputs bore no resemblance to the discourse patterns observed in the real threads. The core problem was Claude's reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) alignment, which conditions the model to produce measured, diplomatic, and politically neutral responses. Real political discourse on Twitter/X is characterised by hostility, partisan attack language, and emotional intensity~\citep{brady2017emotion,antypas2023negativity}. A model trained to avoid producing such content cannot serve as a realistic agent in a simulation of that environment, regardless of how the prompt is structured.

These failures motivated a complete redesign of all three pipeline components.

\section{Phase 2: Agent Classification with Specialised Twitter Models}

\subsection{Model Selection}

The classification stage was redesigned to use five specialised transformer models, each trained on large corpora of Twitter/X data and each targeting a distinct dimension of agent behaviour. We rejected general-purpose language models here because political social-media discourse contains abbreviations, hashtags, irony, and coded language that differ materially from the text distributions on which general-purpose models are mainly trained~\citep{antypas2023negativity,tun2023sentiment}. The trade-off is a more complex model stack. The gain is much stronger domain fit at the point where agent profiles are created.

The five models selected were:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Political Leaning} (matous-volf/political-leaning-politics): classifies text as Left, Centre, or Right along the US political spectrum. Trained on twelve US political datasets spanning news articles, social media posts, and political speeches, achieving an F1 score of 83.6\%. This model was selected over alternatives --- including a SemEval 2016 Hillary Clinton stance detection model tested during development --- because it directly measures ideological position rather than sentiment polarity toward a specific politician.
\item \textbf{Emotion} (cardiffnlp/twitter-roberta-base-emotion): classifies the dominant emotional register of a tweet (anger, joy, sadness, fear, etc.), trained as part of the TweetEval benchmark suite.
\item \textbf{Sentiment} (cardiffnlp/twitter-roberta-base-sentiment-latest): classifies overall sentiment as positive, neutral, or negative. Used both during classification and as the primary validation metric.
\item \textbf{Hate Speech} (cardiffnlp/twitter-roberta-base-hate-latest): outputs a continuous hate speech likelihood score normalised to $[0, 1]$.
\item \textbf{Offensive Language} (cardiffnlp/twitter-roberta-base-offensive): outputs a continuous offensiveness score normalised to $[0, 1]$.
\end{enumerate}

Models 2--5 belong to the CardiffNLP Twitter-RoBERTa family, trained on 124 million tweets collected between 2018 and 2021 and evaluated as part of the TweetEval benchmark~\citep{barbieri2020tweeteval}. We sum the hate and offensive scores into a single aggression parameter for the simulation stage. That simplification loses some nuance between targeted hate and general offensiveness, but it gives a usable scalar control signal for reply probability and tone calibration.

\subsection{The Hillary Stance Model Failure}

During development, we initially tried cardiffnlp/twitter-roberta-base-stance-hillary (trained for SemEval 2016) as the political orientation classifier. It produced heavily skewed outputs: 68.4\% of users were classified as AGAINST, 30.6\% as NONE, and only 1.1\% as FAVOUR, despite the underlying dataset containing 2.5 times more Biden mentions than Trump mentions. Investigation showed that the model detects sentiment polarity toward Hillary Clinton specifically rather than general political ideology. The majority of political tweets are negative in tone regardless of which side they support, so the model classified most content as AGAINST. We therefore replaced it with the political-leaning classifier described above, which directly measures ideological position and produced distributions consistent with broader survey patterns of Twitter/X user behaviour~\citep{smith2021twitterbehaviors,smith2022politicalcontent}.

This failure exposed a broader design rule for the dissertation: domain-specific models still require task-specific validation. We therefore made raw-output inspection and distribution checks mandatory before adopting any classifier in the pipeline.

\subsection{User-Level Aggregation}

Classification was performed at the tweet level, then aggregated to the user level to produce agent DNA profiles. We made that choice because agents represent users, not individual tweets. Over the sampled period, we treat each user's tweets as noisy observations of a relatively stable behavioural profile. In implementation terms, the pipeline groups all classified tweets by user ID, sums simple activity counts such as replies and views, takes the modal label for categorical traits, and takes the arithmetic mean for continuous scores. The simulator then derives a single aggression value by combining the user's mean hate and mean offensive scores, and the prompt builder buckets that value into one of the tone tiers described in Phase~3. This produces one profile row per user and keeps the simulator tied to historical personas rather than isolated posts.

\subsection{Infrastructure}

We ran classification on Google Cloud Platform virtual machine instances equipped with NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPUs. Because the five-model classifier bank was used as a pretrained inference stack rather than fine-tuned within this project, the relevant computational cost is processing time rather than training time. We loaded all five models in a single pass per chunk of data, processing approximately 50,000 tweets per chunk. In practice, each chunk required roughly 20--40 minutes on GPU. This implies a total runtime of about 6--13 hours for the full 20-chunk pass over approximately one million tweets. A checkpoint system saved progress every 1,000 tweets, enabling recovery from interruptions without reprocessing completed work.

\section{Phase 3: Discourse Simulation with an Uncensored Language Model}

\subsection{The Censorship Problem}

Having established a validated agent classification pipeline, the simulation stage still required a language model capable of generating realistic political Twitter discourse. Claude Haiku (\texttt{claude-haiku-4-5-20251001}) was re-evaluated in this role. It produced outputs that were overwhelmingly positive in sentiment (84.8\% positive) even when real threads of the same type were predominantly negative. Prompt engineering alone could not overcome this limitation. RLHF safety alignment is applied during fine-tuning and cannot be removed through instruction. The simulation therefore required a model that could produce the hostile, partisan, and emotionally intense language seen in real political discourse~\citep{brady2017emotion,antypas2023negativity,tun2023sentiment}.

\subsection{Model Selection: Dolphin-Llama3 8B}

We selected Dolphin-Llama3 8B as the simulation model because the main failure of aligned models in this task is not fluency but tone suppression. Dolphin-Llama3 8B is an uncensored fine-tune of Meta's Llama 3 architecture produced by Eric Hartford. Its RLHF safety constraints are relaxed, so the model can generate the hostile and partisan language that aligned models often refuse to produce. The trade-off is obvious. Looser safety controls require tighter research handling. For that reason, all generation remained in a closed research environment. The model was deployed locally via Ollama on a Google Cloud Platform (GCP) NVIDIA T4 GPU instance, which also made iterative tuning in Phase~5 practical.

\subsection{Agent Architecture and Simulation Logic}

Agents were implemented using Mesa, a Python agent-based modelling framework. Each agent was initialised with the DNA profile derived from Phase 2: political leaning, dominant emotion, sentiment, and an aggression score. At each simulation step, agents probabilistically decided whether to post a reply, with base probability of 5\% modified upward by the agent's aggression score. Agents with high aggression scores were more likely to engage and more likely to target ideologically opposing agents, consistent with the Backfire Effect described in the theoretical framework~\citep{diehl2016political,brady2017emotion,composta2025simulating}.

Each agent's system prompt was constructed from its DNA profile, but the crucial design choice was the classifier-to-prompt bridge. We did not hand the generator raw fields such as \texttt{political\_label = Right} or \texttt{aggression = 0.62}. Instead, we translated user-level labels and scores into behavioural descriptions in natural language. This is more than prompt polish. Recent work shows that persona prompting is highly formulation-sensitive: role wording and descriptor choice can materially change alignment, and poorly specified personas can distract the model or produce stereotyped outputs~\citep{kim2024persona,lutz2025prompt}. In other words, the prompt is the mechanism that turns measured profile variables into generator-usable social behaviour.

Political orientation was therefore described in terms of values and vocabulary rather than labels. Left-leaning agents were instructed to support social justice and government accountability, while right-leaning agents were instructed to support limited government and traditional values. We made this choice because small language models (8B parameters) tend to echo categorical labels rather than embody them, producing outputs that explicitly identify themselves as ``a Left-leaning Twitter user'' rather than generating authentic partisan content~\citep{park2023generative}. A scalar such as ``0.62 aggression'' tells the model almost nothing about how to write; a phrase such as ``combative'' or ``casual and conversational'' gives it a behavioural target in the same medium as generation itself.

The behind-the-scenes logic is simple but methodologically important: the modal political label chooses the ideology block, the modal emotion label is looked up in a hand-written behaviour map, and the continuous aggression score is bucketed into one of five tone tiers. Aggression was mapped to five tiers, ranging from ``casual and conversational'' for agents below 0.15 to ``confrontational'' for agents above 0.7. The 0.15/0.3/0.5/0.7 cut points divide the normalised aggression scale into interpretable low, moderate, high, and extreme bands. The extra sub-0.15 tier was added during optimisation so genuinely low-aggression agents could produce non-hostile replies. The later ablation comparing the old four-tier design with the final five-tier design confirmed that this softer tiering improved overall fidelity. The novelty here is not simply that agents have personas, but that classifier-derived ideological, emotional, and aggression signals are verbalised into interpretable prompt fields before generation. That verbalisation layer is part of the method, not just part of the presentation, and is one of the clearest places where this dissertation improves on older prompt formulations.

\section{Phase 4: Validation Framework}

\subsection{Design Rationale}

Validation had to answer a stricter question than ``does the text look plausible?'' It had to determine whether independently generated synthetic threads match real ones closely enough to be useful as a research instrument. We chose Jensen--Shannon Divergence (JSD) as the primary comparison metric because it is symmetric, bounded on $[0, 1]$, and well-defined even when the two distributions assign zero probability to different categories. A JSD of 0 indicates identical distributions. In this dissertation, values below 0.15 are treated as good similarity.

\subsection{Multidimensional Validation}

Initial validation used a single dimension, sentiment, and reported a high accuracy score that later proved misleading. Once we expanded evaluation to five dimensions --- sentiment, political leaning, emotion distribution, aggression level, and keyword content --- the early simulation was shown to produce politically inverted output, near-zero aggression, and replies in which the model identified itself as an AI. Sentiment alone could not detect these failures. A thread can match negative/neutral/positive proportions while still being wrong in politics, tone, and interaction style.

The five-dimensional validation framework classified every generated tweet through the same five RoBERTa models used in the classification stage. This ensured methodological consistency and removed the need for an external oracle. We computed JSD for the sentiment, political, and emotion distributions. We also extracted a continuous sentiment score by computing $P(\text{positive}) - P(\text{negative})$ from the model's raw probability outputs. This enabled paired statistical testing across threads~\citep{antypas2023negativity,tun2023sentiment}. On the final 100-thread batch, this downstream analysis stage processed 12,625 tweets in approximately 11 minutes on a T4 GPU, after which the summary tables and figures were generated.

\subsection{Statistical Testing}

To determine whether any systematic bias existed between simulated and real thread sentiment, a Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied to the per-thread mean sentiment scores (real vs.\ simulated). This non-parametric test was chosen because the distribution of sentiment differences across threads cannot be assumed to be normal. A statistically significant result ($p < 0.05$) indicates that the simulation exhibits a consistent directional bias; a non-significant result indicates that any differences are indistinguishable from random variation.

\section{Phase 5: Parameter Optimisation}

\subsection{Initial Parameter Failure}

A preliminary 100-thread batch validation was run to test whether the redesigned pipeline was viable. It revealed the main remaining defect: systematic negative sentiment bias. The mean per-thread sentiment residual (simulated minus real, on a $[-1, +1]$ scale) was $-0.390 \pm 0.196$, and the Wilcoxon test confirmed that this bias was not random ($p = 4.27 \times 10^{-18}$). In other words, the system was consistently producing more hostile and negative content than the real threads it was meant to simulate. Political distribution and output volume were broadly reasonable, but emotional calibration was still wrong.

Root cause analysis identified several compounding issues. The system prompt instructed all agents to ``match the hostility level of the thread --- Political Twitter is aggressive,'' which imposed a global negativity floor that overrode per-agent aggression calibration. The aggression tier descriptions used extreme language at all levels, meaning even low-aggression agents were instructed to be combative.

\subsection{Prompt Engineering Fixes}

Four coordinated changes were made to address the identified failures:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Softened aggression tone tiers}: The four-tier aggression system was expanded to five tiers and the most extreme language removed. A new lowest tier (aggression $< 0.15$) explicitly permitted casual, conversational, and even agreeable replies, giving the model permission to generate non-negative content for low-aggression agents.
\item \textbf{Rule 6 replacement}: The global hostility instruction (``Political Twitter is aggressive'') was replaced with ``Not every reply is an attack. Sometimes you agree with someone, crack a joke, share a fact, or express genuine concern. Vary your tone naturally.'' This removed the negativity floor that was overriding per-agent calibration.
\item \textbf{Dynamic aggression scaling}: Agent aggression tiers were computed relative to the thread's mean aggression rather than against absolute thresholds. An agent at aggression 0.35 in a mild thread (mean 0.15) was assigned a more assertive tone; the same agent in a hostile thread (mean 0.50) was assigned a calmer tier. This introduced thread-level sensitivity into agent behaviour.
\item \textbf{Expanded context window}: The number of recent tweets in the simulation shown to each agent as conversational context was increased from 3 to 8, providing a more representative sample of the thread's current tone.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Prompting Without Target-Thread Examples}

All simulations in this dissertation were run without providing real reply texts from the target thread to the LLM at generation time. This constraint is methodological, not incidental. It ensures that strong results reflect forward simulation rather than hidden target-thread leakage. Prompt engineering therefore focused only on changes compatible with that constraint, including calibrated aggression tiers, revised behavioural instructions, dynamic aggression scaling, context-window expansion, and vocabulary injection.

\section{Phase 6: Ablation Study}

To identify which design choices most contributed to simulation quality, we conducted a systematic ablation study under a shared constraint: the model received no target-thread reply examples. We tested 21 parameter configurations, each run across 10 diverse threads from the USC dataset (210 total simulations). This constraint was imposed deliberately so every configuration was tested under the same forward-only conditions. Configurations were constructed as single-parameter ablations from the baseline configuration, which changed exactly one parameter at a time, plus five multi-parameter combinations designed to test specific interaction hypotheses.

The full sweep required 42,774 seconds (11.9 hours) on the GCP T4 setup, which is why ablations were evaluated on a deliberately diverse 10-thread subset rather than rerunning the full 100-thread corpus for every parameter change.

Parameters varied included temperature (0.7, 0.9, 1.2), context window size (3, 8, 15 tweets), dynamic aggression scaling (on/off), the Rule 6 formulation (permissive vs.\ hostile), aggression tier count (4 vs.\ 5 tiers), reply probability (2\%, 5\%, 10\%), controversy targeting weight (1.0, 2.5, 5.0), vocabulary injection (on/off), repeat penalty (1.1, 1.3), response length (80 vs.\ 150 tokens), and simulation length (10 vs.\ 20 rounds). The repeat penalty discourages the model from reusing the same words or phrases.

The baseline configuration, with vocabulary injection retained, achieved the highest composite score (0.780). Removing vocabulary injection degraded performance substantially (rank 16, score 0.548). This confirms that the politically charged keyword banks help anchor agent language to authentic partisan discourse. These banks were derived from frequency analysis of the tweet corpus and included concrete terms such as ``MAGA,'' ``Trump,'' and ``Biden.'' The most impactful positive single-parameter changes were expanding the context window from 8 to 15 tweets (rank 6, score 0.713) and increasing temperature from 0.9 to 1.2 (rank 7, score 0.694). The most impactful negative changes were increasing repeat penalty to 1.3 (score 0.429), extending simulation length to 20 rounds (score 0.411), and reducing temperature to 0.7 (score 0.451).

Reply probability was also an influential parameter: halving the base probability from 5\% to 2\% produced the second-best configuration (score 0.777), because lower participation concentrated output among the most motivated agents, improving lexical realism and reducing redundancy. Conversely, doubling reply probability to 10\% improved political alignment but degraded lexical diversity and stance consistency.

\section{Overview}

Across 100 held-out threads, the final pipeline reaches mean overall accuracy of $92.3\% \pm 4.6\%$ and composite fidelity of $0.857 \pm 0.106$, with no significant directional sentiment bias. This chapter explains that result in three stages. Section~\ref{sec:100thread} reports the full 100-thread validation run and shows where the system matches real discourse well and where it fails. Section~\ref{sec:fidelity} then compresses per-thread performance into a composite fidelity score so that the distribution of outcomes can be analysed directly. Section~\ref{sec:ablation_eval} uses the 21-configuration ablation study to show which design choices matter most for fidelity. The discussion section then interprets what the pipeline is actually useful for.

All evaluation was performed against ground-truth data from the USC~X~24 dataset. Simulated threads were generated from the root tweet and agent DNA profiles without access to real reply texts from the target thread. The simulation did not have access to the full set of real thread replies or to the validation metrics of the target thread.

\subsection{Sentiment Bias Analysis}

A critical requirement of the simulation is the absence of systematic directional bias. The mean per-thread sentiment residual (simulated mean minus real mean, on a $[-1, +1]$ scale) was $+0.025 \pm 0.351$. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test yielded $W = 2386.0$, $p = 0.633$, indicating that the observed residuals are statistically indistinguishable from zero. The simulation does not exhibit a systematic tendency to produce more positive or more negative content than the real threads.

This result represents a substantial improvement over the initial pipeline, which exhibited a mean residual of $-0.390$ and a Wilcoxon $p = 4.27 \times 10^{-18}$ (Section~4.5.1). The prompt engineering changes described in Section~4.5.2 successfully eliminated the systematic negative bias.

\subsection{Aggression Calibration}

The Pearson correlation between real and simulated mean aggression scores across threads was $r = 0.332$, $p < 0.001$. We use Pearson here because aggression is treated as a continuous calibration target. The question is whether higher-aggression real threads are matched by proportionally higher-aggression simulated threads, rather than merely preserving rank order. This is a statistically significant positive correlation. Threads with more aggressive real participants tend to produce more aggressive simulated output. The moderate effect size still indicates room for improvement in fine-grained hostility calibration.

\section{Composite Fidelity Score}
\label{sec:fidelity}

To enable a single-number summary of per-thread simulation quality, we constructed a composite fidelity score as a weighted average of five normalised sub-metrics. Each divergence metric was converted to a $[0, 1]$ fidelity score using $1 - \min(v / c, 1)$, where $v$ is the raw metric value and $c$ is a theoretical upper bound (JSD cap $= \ln 2 \approx 0.693$; sentiment residual cap $= 1.0$). Overall accuracy was normalised by dividing by 100. These caps were chosen to place all components on a common scale without arbitrary rescaling. $\ln 2$ is the theoretical upper bound for discrete JSD, and 1.0 is the maximum possible absolute sentiment residual on the $[-1, +1]$ score used here.

The composite score was computed as:
\begin{equation}
\text{Fidelity} = 0.30 \times f_{\text{sent}} + 0.20 \times f_{\text{emo}} + 0.15 \times f_{\text{pol}} + 0.20 \times f_{\text{res}} + 0.15 \times f_{\text{acc}}
\end{equation}
where $f_{\text{sent}}$, $f_{\text{emo}}$, $f_{\text{pol}}$ are the JSD-based fidelity scores for sentiment, emotion, and political leaning respectively, $f_{\text{res}}$ is the absolute sentiment residual fidelity, and $f_{\text{acc}}$ is the normalised overall accuracy.

The weights are deliberately non-uniform. Sentiment JSD receives the largest weight because sentiment calibration was the dominant failure mode in development. The absolute sentiment residual receives a further 0.20 because directionally wrong tone can matter even when categorical sentiment shares appear similar. Emotion receives 0.20 as the main secondary distributional dimension. Political JSD and overall accuracy receive 0.15 each because both remain important but were already comparatively stable. Giving either a larger share would partly double-count information already captured elsewhere.

\subsection{Distribution of Fidelity Scores}

Across 100 threads, the composite fidelity score had a mean of $0.857 \pm 0.106$, with a median of $0.885$. Most threads cluster in the high-fidelity range around 0.80--0.97, with only a small tail of lower-performing cases. Of 100 threads, 45 scored $\geq 0.90$ (excellent fidelity), 79 scored $\geq 0.80$, and only 7 scored below $0.70$. These cut points are descriptive reporting bands rather than universal field standards; they were chosen to separate clearly strong, usable, and weak simulations in an interpretable way.

The best-performing thread (thread\_073, fidelity $= 0.977$) matched the real distribution almost exactly across all five dimensions. The worst-performing thread (thread\_061, fidelity $= 0.431$) exhibited a large sentiment polarity inversion --- the simulation generated predominantly positive content for a thread that was overwhelmingly negative. Sub-metric analysis showed that political JSD fidelity and overall accuracy were consistently high with tight spreads, while absolute sentiment residual varied the most. That makes polarity inversion the dominant failure mode: when a thread's tone is misjudged, the composite score drops sharply.

Figure~\ref{fig:fidelity_ranked} shows all 100 threads ranked by fidelity score, colour-coded by quality tier. The smooth degradation curve confirms that the system does not exhibit bimodal behaviour (i.e., it does not produce either perfect or catastrophic results), but rather degrades gracefully with a long tail of progressively harder threads.

\section{Parameter Sensitivity Analysis}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Summary of Results}

The central research question of this dissertation was whether a simulation pipeline can generate a realistic political reply thread from a root tweet and historically grounded agent profiles. Within the USC~X~24 setting, the answer is \emph{yes, to a useful but bounded degree}. Across 100 held-out threads, mean composite fidelity is $0.857 \pm 0.106$, with 79 threads scoring $\geq 0.80$ (Section~\ref{sec:fidelity}). Sentiment bias is not systematically directional (mean residual $+0.025$, Wilcoxon $p = 0.633$; Section~\ref{sec:100thread}), and political composition is the most accurately reproduced dimension (mean political JSD $= 0.039$). On the separate overall-accuracy metric, 97 of 100 threads are rated EXCELLENT. Taken together, these results show that the pipeline is strong enough to support controlled downstream studies while still leaving visible failure modes that bound the claim.

\subsection{Design and Parameter Justification}

For ABM systems, performance depends as much on behavioural assumptions as on model choice. The strongest design choices in this work are those supported by both mechanism and ablation evidence. First, synchronous activation reduces ordering artefacts by ensuring all agents respond to the same thread snapshot in each round. Second, aggression-calibrated prompting and dynamic scaling improve behavioural realism by making response tone depend on agent profile and thread context rather than enforcing a fixed hostility prior. Third, conversational context length is critical. Very short windows (3 tweets) underperform substantially relative to longer windows, which indicates that local discourse state is necessary for realistic generation. Fourth, higher generation temperature improved realism relative to conservative settings, which suggests that controlled stochasticity is required to match the heterogeneity of real political discourse. Finally, vocabulary injection was confirmed as beneficial by the ablation study. Removing it degraded performance substantially, which indicates that politically charged keyword banks help anchor agent language to authentic partisan discourse.

\subsection{Comparison with Prior Work}

Direct quantitative comparison with other LLM-based social simulation systems remains limited because studies use different targets, metrics, and datasets. The Italian election simulation~\citep{composta2025simulating} reports coalition-level interaction fidelity, whereas this dissertation evaluates thread-level distributional match across multiple dimensions. The two should therefore be read as complementary evidence for the broader feasibility of LLM-based social simulation rather than as benchmark-equivalent scores. Recent work on cross-partisan reply dynamics likewise shows that discourse outcomes depend strongly on audience composition and interaction context~\citep{cetinkaya2025crosspartisan}. This is consistent with the sensitivity patterns observed in the present ablation study.

Traditional opinion dynamics models~\citep{deffuant2000mixing,hegselmann2002opinion} provide mathematical frameworks for opinion evolution, but they do not produce text. This makes direct metric comparison impossible. The key advantage of the present LLM-based approach is that it generates natural-language output that can be evaluated using the same automated tools applied to real social media data. That enables empirical validation that purely mathematical models cannot support~\citep{composta2025simulating,butler2024misinformation}.

\subsection{Research-Enabling Case Study: Re-testing Moral-Emotional Diffusion Claims}

The previous sections show that the pipeline can reproduce real reply-thread distributions to a useful degree, and that its strongest design choices are supported by ablation evidence. That is necessary, but not sufficient, for the main dissertation claim. If the system is a research instrument rather than only a validated generator, it must support a type of study that retrospective methods cannot run cleanly. The purpose of this case study is to show that payoff directly. Once the simulator is validated, we can hold audience composition fixed, edit the root tweet in a controlled way, and observe how downstream discourse changes.

We demonstrate that payoff by revisiting a well-known result from observational political-communication research. \citet{brady2017emotion} analyse 563,312 tweets across climate change, gun control, and same-sex marriage, using observational models of retweet diffusion. Their central finding is that moral-emotional language is associated with greater spread, with an average 20\% increase in retweet rate per additional moral-emotional word (mean incidence rate ratio, IRR = 1.20). They also report topic-specific patterns, such as positive moral-emotional effects for same-sex marriage and stronger negative-valence effects in climate discourse~\citep{brady2017emotion}.

The value of re-testing that claim here is methodological, not adversarial. Brady et al.\ identify an important regularity, but the observational design necessarily compares \emph{different} tweets from \emph{different} users, audiences, and moments in time. That makes it difficult to isolate what follows from wording alone, as opposed to author identity, audience composition, or timing. The present pipeline can remove most of that variation. It therefore lets us ask a different question: if one word in the root tweet changes, while the audience and simulation assumptions stay fixed, how much does the downstream reply thread change?

The case-study design is deliberately simple. We selected one root tweet for each of three Brady topics: climate, gun, and marriage. For each root tweet, we ran three conditions: the original control tweet, one positive one-word edit, and one negative one-word edit. This produced 9 simulated threads in total (3 topics $\times$ 3 conditions). All runs used the same 10-round simulation horizon, the same prompting setup, and no target-thread reply examples. Within each topic, all three conditions reused the same thread-matched agent population constructed from the historical tweets of users in the original thread. The only manipulated variable was the single lexical substitution in the root tweet.

The edits were chosen to be transparent rather than clever. In the climate tweet, ``commitments is forming'' became ``commitments is working'' or ``commitments is failing.'' In the gun tweet, ``Do you hate the fact that women hunt...'' became ``Do you love the fact that women hunt...'' or ``Do you detest the fact that women hunt...'' In the marriage tweet, ``I know it's good luck...'' became ``I know it's great luck...'' or ``I know it's bad luck...'' These are intentionally minimal interventions. The experiment changes one word already present in the sentence rather than adding a new slogan, hashtag, or clause.

Under those controlled conditions, small wording changes often produced large downstream differences. Across the 6 edited conditions, the mean absolute shift in thread-level continuous sentiment was 0.443 on the $[-1, +1]$ scale (median $= 0.309$). All 6 edits changed sentiment by more than 0.10 relative to the control condition. Reply volume also moved. The mean absolute change was 8.2 replies (median $= 8$), and 3 of the 6 edits changed participation by at least 10 replies. The main result is therefore not a simple ``positive word in, positive replies out'' rule. It is that even a one-word change can materially alter the thread that forms around a post when the audience is held fixed.

The topic-level examples make that point more clearly. In the climate family, the control tweet about climate commitments ``forming'' produced a mildly positive thread-level mean sentiment of $+0.150$. Changing ``forming'' to ``working'' pushed the simulated thread negative ($-0.174$), reduced the number of replies from 90 to 87, and increased the negative-share of replies from 22.2\% to 49.4\%. Changing ``forming'' to ``failing'' also pushed the thread negative ($-0.144$), but this time increased reply volume to 101 posts. The climate example shows that even two edits with similar lexical direction can produce different structural effects: both made the thread more negative, but only one increased participation.

The gun family is even clearer. The control tweet containing ``hate'' produced a near-neutral mean sentiment of $+0.049$ with a mixed reply distribution (37.7\% negative, 40.6\% positive). Changing ``hate'' to ``love'' raised mean sentiment sharply to $+0.580$, increased replies from 106 to 111, and shifted the reply mix to 74.8\% positive and only 1.8\% negative. By contrast, changing ``hate'' to ``detest'' did \emph{not} make the thread more negative. It still raised mean sentiment to $+0.198$, while reducing participation from 106 to 88 replies. This is a useful counterexample. It shows why the case study matters: the downstream effect of a lexical change cannot be read off directly from dictionary-level valence.

The marriage family gives the strongest example of nonlinearity. The control tweet with ``good luck'' produced an overwhelmingly positive thread ($+0.918$ mean sentiment; 97.7\% positive replies). Changing ``good'' to ``bad'' lowered mean sentiment to $+0.737$ and increased replies slightly from 87 to 88. That is directionally intuitive. But changing ``good'' to ``great'' produced the most dramatic reversal in the entire case study: mean sentiment collapsed from $+0.918$ to $-0.261$, reply volume rose from 87 to 98, and the reply mix flipped from 97.7\% positive to 62.2\% negative. This is exactly the kind of result that observational designs struggle to isolate. The edit was lexically simple and superficially more positive, yet the downstream thread became substantially more negative.

These examples show why the case study should not be read as a claim that one-word edits have universal effects. The point is almost the opposite. Once audience composition is held fixed, small wording changes can trigger topic-specific and nonlinear downstream dynamics. In some cases the edit changes tone more than participation. In others it changes participation more than tone. In still others, a more positive word produces a more negative thread. That context sensitivity is not a failure of the instrument. It is the substantive phenomenon that the instrument is designed to expose.

This comparison therefore shows where the pipeline changes the methodological question. Brady et al.\ establish observational diffusion regularities at scale. The present system adds controlled, within-message counterfactual testing. Instead of only asking which language features correlate with spread in heterogeneous real-world data, we can ask what changes when one word is edited while audience composition and behavioural assumptions are held constant. That same design pattern is useful beyond this example. It can support research replications, communication experiments, and bounded product-side message testing, provided the target population remains close to the evaluated setting.

\subsection{Failure Modes}

The dominant failure mode is sentiment polarity inversion. In 7 of 100 threads (fidelity $< 0.70$), generated sentiment moves in the opposite direction to the real thread. These cases appear to arise when the root tweet alone is an insufficient predictor of downstream tone (e.g., rallying, ironic, or humour-driven replies). A second weakness is partial hostility miscalibration: although aggression tracks real threads in the correct direction, the effect size is moderate ($r = 0.332$), indicating that tone intensity is captured only coarsely. Taken together, these errors indicate that the model is strongest on macro-distributional realism and weaker on fine-grained affective trajectory prediction.

\subsection{Limitations}

The limitations can be interpreted as four threats to validity. \textit{Construct validity}: all outcomes are measured through automated classifiers, so any systematic classifier bias directly affects both error estimates and apparent agreement. \textit{Internal validity}: several behavioural mechanisms (bounded confidence threshold, aggression scaling, round limit) are hand-specified and tuned, so alternative yet plausible formulations could yield different outcomes. \textit{External validity}: evaluation uses one dataset (USC~X~24) from one platform and one election context, so transfer to other political systems, languages, or platform affordances remains unverified. \textit{Analytical validity}: composite fidelity depends on chosen metric weights and normalisation caps, so different weighting schemes may change model rankings even when raw sub-metrics are unchanged. A practical limitation is compute budget. The 100-thread validation and 21-configuration ablation were already expensive, and with more time and compute we would likely test larger held-out sets, finer parameter grids, and more threshold variants. The same constraint applies to model comparison. Qwen2.5~7B was evaluated using the parameter set optimised for Dolphin-Llama3~8B, not a Qwen-specific sweep, so the current cross-model ranking should be read as a comparison under shared settings rather than a definitive best-possible result for both models. With more compute, a dedicated Qwen ablation could plausibly improve its performance and potentially change the ranking. These constraints do not invalidate the present findings, but they bound the claims. This work provides strong evidence of feasibility in a defined setting rather than a universal model of online reaction dynamics.

A further boundary condition is persona conditioning. Agent behaviour is conditioned on historically aggregated user profiles derived from prior tweets, and the strongest results should therefore be interpreted as \emph{conditional fidelity under history-informed agent profiles}. This supports a meaningful forward-simulation claim in the USC~X~24 setting, but not a claim that the model captures general human behaviour independently of persona information. Establishing that stronger claim requires explicit counterfactual baselines (e.g., demographic-matched random agents and no-persona agents) evaluated under identical protocols.

\chapter{Conclusions}

\section{Conclusion}
This dissertation asked whether a forward-looking simulation pipeline can generate realistic political reply threads from only a root tweet and historically grounded agent profiles. The results show that it can, to a useful but bounded degree. Across 100 held-out threads, the optimised Dolphin-Llama3~8B pipeline achieved mean JSD values of 0.086 (sentiment), 0.039 (political), and 0.118 (emotion), with mean overall accuracy of $92.3\% \pm 4.6\%$ and composite fidelity of $0.857 \pm 0.106$. Sentiment bias was not statistically directional (mean residual $+0.025$, Wilcoxon $p = 0.633$). This indicates that the final system no longer exhibits the strong negative drift observed in the initial configuration.

Methodologically, the main contribution is a new research instrument: an end-to-end ABM+LLM pipeline that can run forward and counterfactual thread-level experiments rather than only retrospective engagement analyses. The case study shows why that matters. Once the pipeline is validated, it can be used to revisit claims from observational work under fixed audience composition and controlled lexical changes. The same capability is also relevant to bounded pre-post message testing, where the question is not ``will this go viral?'' but ``how might discourse shift if the wording changes?''

The contribution is not a universal model of online political behaviour. It is a validated simulation framework with clear operational boundaries, supported by multi-metric evaluation, ablation evidence, and cross-model comparison. It enables previously hard-to-test questions about downstream discourse effects. The ablation and model-comparison results are especially informative for future systems design. Performance proved sensitive to context length, generation temperature, reply probability, and vocabulary conditioning, while cross-model transfer showed that sentiment and aggression calibration are model-dependent whereas political-distribution alignment is comparatively stable. These findings turn implementation choices into testable design guidance rather than ad hoc prompt heuristics.

\section{Future Directions}
Future work should now move from demonstrating feasibility within the USC~X~24 setting to testing how far the instrument generalises and what, exactly, drives its performance. The first priority is to separate the effect of history-informed personas from the rest of the pipeline by running matched demographic-random and no-persona baselines under the same protocol. The second is external validity. The current evaluation should be extended beyond a single US election-period dataset to non-election intervals, non-US political contexts, and multilingual settings. A third direction is to improve temporal and structural realism by modelling event time and platform-specific interaction constraints, ideally against reconstructed reply-chain traces rather than flat thread summaries. Because all headline metrics depend on automated classifiers, future evaluations should also propagate classifier uncertainty into fidelity estimates and supplement those estimates with blinded human judgements of realism, stance plausibility, and conversational coherence. Taken together, these steps would turn the present result from a strong setting-specific demonstration into a more general and causally interpretable instrument for studying online political discourse.
